The Bee Network

How Greater Manchester's buses came back under public control, told from the mayoral record

A sourced narrative of Greater Manchester's Bee Network, drawn from the GMCA mayoralty material in this archive. Every substantive claim cites a specific record in raw/gmca/. Where the corpus does not support a claim, this page marks the gap rather than fills it.

The Bee Network is the branding for Andy Burnham's integrated, London-style public transport system across the ten boroughs of Greater Manchester. It combines a franchised, publicly-controlled bus network, the Metrolink tram system, cycling and walking infrastructure, a region-wide bike hire scheme, and (in time) integrated rail. It is the largest extension of public control over an English city-region's transport system since the deregulation of the 1980s.

Origins of the policy

The Bee Network framing was unveiled during Burnham's first mayoral term (2017 to 2021) but accelerated decisively after his re-election in May 2021. On 10 May 2021, the newly re-elected Mayor announced the establishment of the Bee Network Delivery Board and a package of measures intended to "accelerate plans for an improved, London-style transport network" (source). Chris Boardman was appointed Transport Commissioner eleven days later (source).

Burnham positioned the Bee Network throughout as a single-system replacement for the fragmented, deregulated arrangements that had governed Greater Manchester's buses since 1986. The recurring framing across the GMCA corpus is that London-level integration and pricing have historically driven higher patronage and economic productivity, and that Greater Manchester should have the same.

Bus franchising and the London-style argument

The decision to bring Greater Manchester's buses under public control was the structural centrepiece of the Bee Network. It was the first such decision by any city-region outside London since the Transport Act 1985 deregulated bus services. The formal process started on 8 December 2021 with a business exercise to recruit operators for services in Bolton and Wigan (source).

The "London-style" framing appears repeatedly across the GMCA records: in the 10 May 2021 announcement, in the 14-15 March 2022 acceleration press release (source), in the 25 July 2022 statement on the franchising judicial review (source), and in the September 2023 ticket launch (source). It is the most consistent rhetorical thread Burnham deploys on transport across the entire corpus.

Burnham's substantive claim about franchising is set out clearly in his 29 October 2024 statement on the national £2 bus cap (source). It rests on three points. First, the per-kilometre cost of the new franchised contracts is on average one third cheaper than tenders under the old deregulated system. Second, taking control of the farebox means that any profits from rising patronage are recycled into keeping fares low rather than retained by private operators. Third, control of the system enables further actions to help residents (the example given is annual Bee Bus Passes made available through Greater Manchester's 14 credit unions on weekly payments, claimed to cut weekly bus travel costs by around £5).

Legal and political obstacles

The principal legal obstacle was a judicial review brought by bus operator Rotala plc challenging the lawfulness of the franchising decision. In March 2022 the High Court dismissed the challenge and found the decision lawful in all respects. Rotala appealed; the Court of Appeal unanimously dismissed the appeal on 25 July 2022 and awarded costs to GMCA (source). Burnham's statement that day described the appeal as "without any merit" and the original decision as "lawful and right", and explicitly framed the judgment as paving the way for other city-regions such as Liverpool City Region and South Yorkshire.

The political obstacles included continuing operator resistance, central-government wariness about transferring further transport powers, and the perennial Greater Manchester complaint that the city-region's devolution settlement remained narrower than London's. The 17 December 2024 exchange of letters with the Chancellor and Deputy Prime Minister on the English Devolution White Paper (source, source) is the most direct evidence in the corpus of the funding-and-powers conversation that has run alongside delivery throughout.

Rollout phases

The rollout was announced as three phases in the 14-15 March 2022 acceleration package (source). The dates broadly held, with a small slip on the final phase.

Phase 1 went live on 24 September 2023, covering Wigan, Bolton, parts of Salford and west Manchester. Fifty zero-emission buses entered service as part of an initial order of 220 (source). The launch coincided with the introduction of new Bee AnyBus and tram tickets, claimed to make multi-modal travel around twenty percent cheaper than buying products separately.

Phase 2 went live in March 2024, covering Bury, Rochdale, Oldham and parts of north Manchester. By that point fifty percent of bus services in Greater Manchester were under public control.

Phase 3 went live overnight on 4 to 5 January 2025, covering Stockport, Trafford, Tameside, south Manchester and the remaining parts of Salford (source). At that point the entire Greater Manchester bus network was under public control, ending forty years of deregulation. The 29 December 2024 New Year message described this as "the most visible piece of successful public service delivery in recent times" and as proof that things "can indeed be fixed, moved forward and made to work better".

Integration of buses, trams, walking, cycling and rail

The corpus is clearest on bus-and-tram integration. The September 2023 launch of the Bee AnyBus and tram tickets (source) provided unlimited bus plus tram travel for as little as £5.40, with the full Metrolink network on an all-day ticket at £7.80 off-peak or £9.50 peak. The Bee Network app served as the integrated retail channel.

Cycling and walking integration was set out alongside the May 2021 acceleration announcement: 100km of cycling and walking routes were committed for completion by the end of 2021, a Greater Manchester Bike Hire scheme launched in November 2021, and 95 electric vehicle charging points were committed by end-2021 with a further 200 in 2022 (source).

Rail integration has been more difficult and is the weakest part of the integrated story. The September 2023 ticket launch announced a contactless touch-in/touch-out pilot on local train services, claimed as the first such pilot outside London. On 31 October 2024 the Mayor and other northern mayors wrote to the Chancellor and Transport Secretary warning that "unacceptable" rail service from operator Northern was "hindering growth in the North of England", citing 1,600 train cancellations in the half-term week (source). The Rail North Committee, which Burnham chairs, summoned the operator to an emergency meeting on 30 October 2024.

A contactless London-style payment system with daily and weekly caps was announced for 23 March 2025 in the 29 October 2024 statement (source).

Funding and devolution links

The Bee Network's funding sits at the intersection of GMCA budgets, central government grants and farebox income.

The mayoral budget for 2023-24 was approved on 27 January 2023 (source), with Bee Network delivery costs included in the precept and transport-grant lines.

The trailblazer devolution deal of 2023 expanded GMCA's powers and funding flexibility, and the December 2024 English Devolution White Paper was the first major step of the new government's devolution settlement. The Mayor's response to the White Paper on 17 December 2024 (source) flagged remaining gaps in transport funding parity with London.

The 29 October 2024 statement on the £2 cap (source) is the corpus's clearest description of the financial logic the Mayor attaches to public control: per-kilometre contract costs one third cheaper than the deregulated system, farebox proceeds recycled rather than distributed, and credit-union-supported annual passes as a residents'-side innovation.

Burnham's main claims about the Bee Network

Drawing only from the corpus, Burnham makes the following substantive claims about the Bee Network:

On public control: the Bee Network ends four decades of bus deregulation in Greater Manchester (source) and makes Greater Manchester the first city-region outside London to bring buses fully back under public control (source).

On fares: the £2 single (£1 child) and £5 day cap (£2.50 child) introduced on 1 September 2022 contributed to an estimated twelve percent increase in bus trips (source). The new tickets launched in September 2023 made multi-modal bus and tram travel around twenty percent cheaper than buying separately.

On cost-effectiveness: the new franchised contracts are on average one third cheaper per kilometre than under deregulation (source).

On reliability: in the 29 December 2024 New Year message (source) the Mayor states that Bee Network services have "settled, are better than those they replaced and improving all the time" after early challenges.

On accountability: a single-system, public-controlled network means residents can hold the Mayor and GMCA accountable for service standards rather than fragmented private operators (source).

Criticisms, risks and delivery problems

The corpus contains relatively little direct criticism, since GMCA press releases are first-party material. But three substantive risks are visible.

First, the Rotala judicial review was the main legal risk. It was defeated comprehensively in 2022 (source) but it slowed the original timetable by months and shaped the cautious, phase-by-phase rollout approach.

Second, rail integration has been visibly difficult. The 31 October 2024 letter on Northern's performance is the most explicit corpus statement that the system the Bee Network is supposed to integrate with is itself unreliable (source). A fully London-style Bee Network requires the rail leg to work; on the evidence in the corpus, the rail leg has been the weakest link.

Third, the 29 December 2024 message explicitly anticipates "a bumpy few days" during the south Manchester transition in early January 2025. The corpus does not contain the follow-up records that would tell us how the transition actually went; this is a clear gap.

The corpus also does not contain critical material from passenger groups, opposition political figures, the bus operators' own perspective, or independent academic assessment. Any thorough analysis of the Bee Network's first eighteen months would draw on those sources too. They are not in the present vault.

Marked gaps

Three substantive gaps in the corpus are flagged for follow-up:

First, no records cover the actual transition experience in any of the three phases beyond press-release framing. To assess how the rollout went in practice, contemporary passenger-side coverage (Greater Manchester press, BBC North West, passenger group reports) would need to be ingested.

Second, the trailblazer devolution deal text is not in the corpus. The 2023 devolution deal between GMCA and HM Treasury is referenced in passing but not held as a source document. A targeted ingest of the deal text would strengthen the funding-and-devolution section.

Third, no opposition, operator or academic critique is represented. The archive as currently constituted is built from Burnham-led and government-led primary sources. A balanced briefing would benefit from a curated set of contrary sources; those are outside the present corpus.

These gaps do not prevent the page from being complete as a first-party narrative. They mark where the second pass would add adversarial and independent material if needed.
